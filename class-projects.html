Mechanical Engineering Senior Design

In a group with four other students with support from our sponsor and isntructor, we were tasked with creating a novel sensing technology for strain and temperature by leveraging additive manufacturing to produce an optical waveguide with embedded colloidal quantum dots. Shining light of a certain wavelengths "activates" the quantum dots, absorbing the light and outputing light of a different wavelength. Heating up or applying strain to the medium with quantum dots changes their output wavelength. By measuring that wavelength we can then calculate the temperature and strain difference that is being applied. Below shows how we accomplished that goal. See our paper published in the IEEE Sensors journal here: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11614448. Check out our article published by APL! https://me.jhu.edu/news/student-engineers-invent-bendable-temperature-sensor-to-avoid-overheated-devices/

Custom Printing Nozzle

To print our sensor out of QD-embedded silicone, I designed a multi-nozzle print head to switch between materials, and leveraged resin printing for its tight tolerances.

Additively Manufactured Sensor

We used volumetric and pressure extrusion methods on a 3D printer to manufacture the sensor, using G-code to control positions and feed rates. Extensive material compatibility testing was done to determine the material for each waveguide layer.

Test Setups

I designed the following test setups to control the temperature of the sensor using a thermo-electric cooler, and the strain is varied using a linear stage micrometer, mimicing a tensile test.

Solid Mechanics in Extreme Environments: Project

To better understand how structures behave in extreme environments, my group was tasked with applying theory to a real world scenario. We = decided to study the Apollo missions' heat shield. To understand the stress and deformation on the system, I employed disk/plate deformation theory, linear thermal expansion, and numerical methods to model the multilayered shield. I then moved onto ANSYS mechanical, as it gave me the flexibility to create custom aerospace materials and model each individual layer. I conducted a mesh analysis, comparing the results from a default and tetrahedral mesh, adjusting the adaptive size resolution, and verifying mesh validity using quality metrics. A combination of Steady-Sate Thermal and Static Structural was used to model the heat shield.

Applied Computational Modeling in Aerodynamics and Heat Transfer

Applied Computational Modeling taught students the fundamentals of fluid modeling using ANSYS CFX, modeling systems as simple as flow over a cylinder, to a complex system like a heat exchanger.

Flow Over a Cylinder/Airfoil

  • Flow over cylinder: Varied parameters and domain size to see how it impacts results for velocity/pressure
  • Flow over airfoil: Varied angle of attack and recorded results for velocity/pressure

Final Project: Immersion Cooling System

Modeled after an immersion cooling system, the temperature distribution and heat exchange inside of the system is recorded as a function of the amount of bends in the system. Combination of an inflation mesh on the surfaces and tetrahedral was used to improve validity of the simulation. Heat exchange system was isolated to limit simulation time. The results were then analyzed and discussed.

Heat Transfer Lab Heat Exchanger

Description

Teams of 3 must design, build, and test a heat exchanger that connects to a pre-built testing rig. The design must incorporate female 1/2 NPT threaded connects to integrate into test rig. With feedback from Baltimore Air Coil Engineers, produce a heat exchanger that follows conventional models.

How it works/Design Considerations

Heat Exchanger Follows a Shell-and-Tube model, with cold water flowing through PVC outer shell, and hot water flowing through copper pipes inside the of the shell. To facilitate heat transfer, fins are attached along the copper pipes, increasing the area of heat transfer and increasing cold water turbulence to improve heat transfer. Heat Exchanger performance is estimated using a MATLAB model.

Mechanics-Based Design Lab Crane

Description

In teams of 3, Design and construct a crane capable of lifting a 10-pound weight off the floor by 1 inch within a time span of 2 minutes. Teams must use a DC micro gearmotor provided by the instructor, the crane must be mounted onto a mounting plate with given dimensions, the boom arm cannot be constructed out of metal, counterweights cannot be used, and with a given budget of $25 (materials can only be purchased from McMaster-Carr and Amazon).

How it works/Design Considerations

The provided motor is connected to a gearbox which contains a compound gear ratio that increases the output torque of the system to lift the weight. The output shaft in the gearbox has a spool where a steel cable is attached and fed across the boom arm. A pulley at the end of the boom arm redirects the wire and connects to the weight. The boom arm is made of a 3/8” thick sheet of acrylic, and the rest of the plates are made of ¼” acrylic sheets, including the pulley. All critical dimensions were determined using beam deflection calculations, gear ratio calculations, and safety/design factors. To save material, the shafts in the gearbox had a hex profile, removing the need for a pin to secure the gears.

Construction

The Plates were all assembled using ¼-20 screws and nuts. Gearbox shafts were cut on a bandsaw and the ends were turned down to allow rotation. All plates were laser cut, and the groove in the pulley was made on a manual lathe using a chamfer tool. Spacers for the gearbox were laser cut out of 1/16” thick acrylic.

Manufacturing Engineering Sterling Engine

Description

For the Manufacturing Engineering Lab I had to manufacture all of the parts of a Sterling engine by hand. I was provided with the engineering drawings for each part, and I had to manufacture each part using the manual mill, manual lathe, shears, hole punches and sheet metal benders. I assembled the Sterling engine using super glue and rivets, and determined the proper arm length (yellow brass pieces) that would enable the engine to run when heat is applied to the glass tube. By the end of the semester, I had produced the fastest running engine in my class due to my optimal stroke length and minimal friction.
